Most students think the financial part of a visa application is a number. Get the balance high enough, print the statement, done.

Officers are running a different test. They are asking whose money it is, where it came from, and whether it will still be there next year. A student with less money and a clean story gets through more often than a student with more money and an unexplained deposit.

## Who is allowed to fund you

The usual accepted sponsors are you, your parents, and in many cases a close blood relative. Beyond that it gets country-specific and it gets harder.

Parents are the default and the easiest to document. A sibling or uncle funding you is common in Bangladesh and generally workable, but expect more questions and prepare more paperwork, including proof of the relationship itself.

A family friend, your employer, or a fiancé is where applications get refused. Some countries simply do not accept a non-relative sponsor for a student visa, and even where it is allowed the officer will want a reason that holds up. "He is like an uncle to me" is not a relationship an immigration officer can file.

Official scholarships and government sponsorship sit in their own category and are usually the easiest of all, because the awarding body issues a letter that answers every question at once.

Check the rule for your specific country before you build a plan around a particular sponsor. This is one of the areas where the answer genuinely differs between the UK, Canada and Australia.

## Source of funds is the real test

This is the part Bangladeshi applicants underestimate, and it is where most of our fixing work happens.

Having the money is step one. Explaining how it got into the account is step two, and step two is where files fall apart. A balance that jumped by twenty lakh six weeks ago, with no explanation, reads as borrowed for the application. Officers see this constantly, because it happens constantly.

What a clean source-of-funds story looks like: salary credited monthly over a long period, with matching pay slips and a job letter. Rental income with a tenancy agreement. A land or flat sale with the deed, the registration documents and the proceeds arriving as one traceable transfer. A business with a trade licence, tax returns and bank flow that matches the income you claim.

What it does not look like: several large cash deposits, a transfer from an account belonging to someone not named in your application, or a sudden balance with a one-line affidavit attached.

## If you are selling land to fund this

Many families do, and it is perfectly acceptable. It just has to be documented properly rather than turning up as an unexplained lump.

Keep the sale deed, the mutation and registration papers, and evidence that the buyer's payment moved into your sponsor's account by a traceable route. If the money came as cash and was then deposited, expect to explain that, and expect the explanation to matter more than the amount.

The timing matters too. Selling the land and applying the same month is the weakest version of this. Selling it, banking it properly, and letting it sit is much stronger.

## The affidavit is not magic

Bangladeshi applicants lean heavily on a notarised affidavit of support, and it does have a role. It formalises the sponsor's commitment.

But an affidavit proves only that somebody signed something. It does not prove the money exists, that it is theirs, or where it came from. On its own it carries very little weight. Attach it to real bank history and real income evidence, or it is a piece of paper.

## Sponsor documents worth collecting early

Your sponsor's bank statements covering a decent stretch of history, not just the last month. Their proof of income, whether that is salary slips and a job letter, trade licence and tax returns, or rental agreements. Their national ID and passport copies. Evidence of your relationship to them, such as birth certificates showing shared parents, or a family certificate.

Get these in one folder before you start, because chasing an uncle for a tax return two days before a deadline is its own kind of stress.

## The mistake that causes the most refusals

Borrowing money to sit in an account for the application, then returning it. Officers know this pattern intimately. It is the single most common reason strong students from Bangladesh get refused, and when it is detected the damage carries into the next application because the credibility question follows you.

If the money is not genuinely available for your studies, the application is not ready. Wait an intake and do it properly. That is a real answer, even when it is the one nobody wants.
