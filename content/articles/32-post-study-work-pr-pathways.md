A lot of students tell us they want to "settle there" while choosing a course that quietly makes it impossible. Post-study work rights depend on what you study, where, and at what level. By the time you graduate, those choices are locked.

If staying matters to you, treat it as an entry requirement for your shortlist rather than something to sort out later.

## Canada

The most designed route of the lot. The post-graduation work permit leads into permanent residence through the points-based system, where Canadian study and Canadian work experience both score.

The catches are at the front. The proof-of-funds bar is high at CAN$23,448 a year for a single applicant, plus tuition. And eligibility for the work permit depends on your institution and programme, so a course at the wrong college can leave you with a degree and no work rights. Confirm this before you accept an offer, not after.

## Australia

A genuine pathway, with post-study work rights that vary by qualification level and sometimes by where in the country you studied, since regional study has carried extra benefits.

Permanent residence runs through skilled migration and depends heavily on your occupation being in demand. That list changes. An occupation that scores well today may not in three years, which is the main risk in planning an Australian route a long way ahead.

## United Kingdom

The Graduate route lets you stay and work after completing an eligible course. It is a window rather than a pathway: you get time to find skilled employment, and the move to longer-term status depends on landing a job with a licensed sponsor at the required salary.

That sponsorship requirement is the real filter. Plenty of graduates spend the window job-hunting and go home. The UK suits students confident of being employable in a sponsor-heavy sector.

UK policy in this area has moved repeatedly and quickly. Of all the destinations here, this is the one where a plan made three years out is least safe.

## Germany and parts of Europe

Underrated for this. Very low public tuition, a post-study job-seeking period, and a route onward from employment.

The obstacles are language and qualification recognition rather than money. English-taught degrees are common, but the graduate job market often is not English-speaking, and students who skip German find the work permit theoretical.

## Malaysia and the UAE

Be clear-eyed. Neither is a realistic permanent-residency route for most students. Both can be excellent for a degree, for regional employment and for cost, and Malaysia in particular works well as a first step that strengthens a later application elsewhere.

If someone tells you a Malaysian degree leads to Malaysian citizenship, they are selling you something.

## How to choose if staying is the goal

Work backwards. Decide the country, check which qualification levels and institutions keep post-study work rights open, then choose the course from that filtered list.

Check whether your intended occupation is wanted there, not just whether you will enjoy the subject. Skilled migration systems are blunt about which jobs they need.

Assume the rules will change while you study, because they will. Prefer routes where you have a fallback, such as a degree that is also valuable at home, over ones that only make sense if a specific policy survives four years.

Be realistic about the job market you will be competing in as a graduate with no local experience and a visa that needs sponsoring. The students who succeed usually started building local experience, internships and networks in their first year, not after graduation.

## The honest summary

Canada has the clearest path and the highest entry cost. Australia is strong but depends on occupation lists that move. The UK gives you a window and then a hard employer test. Germany is cheap and real if you learn the language. Malaysia and the UAE are good places to study and not routes to settlement.
